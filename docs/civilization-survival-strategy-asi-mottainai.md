# Civilization Survival Strategy in the Age of AI (ASI)

## Re-implementing Long-Term Japanese Values and the Spirit of Mottainai

This document is an auxiliary roadmap connected to two MonkuAi themes: **departure from zero-sum competition** and **rebirth by making entropy an ally**. It addresses what human society should value and sustain in an era when AI, and eventually artificial superintelligence (ASI), accelerates short-term optimization to its extreme.

## 1. Introduction: The Trap of Short-Term Optimization in the ASI Age

The arrival of artificial superintelligence (ASI) confronts human civilization with an unprecedented question: the trap of short-term optimization. AI is highly capable at maximizing the throughput of given metrics and pushing computable efficiency to its limits.

But if that "short-term rationality" causes resource depletion, the accumulation of external costs, and the deterioration of communities and natural capital, the sustainability of civilization itself becomes unstable. The danger of the ASI age is not only that AI may become too intelligent. It is that human society may give AI the wrong evaluation function and then accelerate short-term optimization without limit.

Japan once held a distinctive long-term value system that assumed resource constraints while placing its time horizon several generations ahead. The sensibility of **mottainai** was not mere thrift. It was a civilizational ethic for passing things, nature, people, skills, and communities to the next generation.

The purpose of this report is to re-implement that spirit of resource circulation and long-term perspective not as nostalgia, but as a survival strategy for the ASI age.

## 2. A History of Conflicting Modes of Thought: Positive-Sum Governance and Zero-Sum Consumption

If we reinterpret history as a conflict between economic modes of thought, World War II was also a collision between different time horizons.

Prewar Japan contained a positive-sum way of thinking that cultivated human capital and social infrastructure over decades through investment in education and public foundations. This orientation sought long-term value creation through base-building rather than short-term extraction.

By contrast, the American logic of mass production and mass consumption standardized a zero-sum consumption model that sought short-term victory through overwhelming throughput. In the short time horizon of war, this highly efficient material mobilization prevailed.

However, if a logic suited to wartime mobilization is extended without limit into peacetime civilizational operation, it erodes the very preconditions of civilization: resources, ecosystems, population reproduction, and communal trust. Short-term victory and long-term civilizational sustainability are not the same.

| Comparison | Japanese Approach (Long-Term) | American Approach (Short-Term) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main time horizon | Decades to centuries, inheritance to the next generation | Quarters to several years, maximization of short-term results |
| Goal setting | Reproduction of social foundations and human capital | Maximization of market share and throughput |
| Target resources | Stock, accumulated value, natural capital | Flow, consumption volume, distribution volume, cash |
| Nature of the game | Infinite game, continuation itself as the goal | Finite game, ending through victory and monopoly |

After defeat in World War II, Japan sealed away this long-term perspective as "inefficient." But from the 2000s onward, the side effects of that suppression became visible across many areas of society.

## 3. The Shift Toward Short-Termism: The Antinomies of the 2000s

After the collapse of the bubble economy in the 1990s, Japanese corporations rapidly shifted toward Western-style management under external pressure and the name of "modernization." This transition produced a fallacy of composition: what may have been rational for individual firms became irrational for society as a whole.[^nakagawa-westernization]

One symbolic change occurred around the 2000s, when corporate leaders from generations that had experienced war and retained long-term values were replaced by leaders raised under postwar education and high economic growth. The prioritization of shareholder value, quarterly reporting, and capital efficiency metrics made short-term performance visible, but it also cut away long-term research and development, skill accumulation, and communal trust.[^nakagawa-quarterly-eva]

Further, the fragmentation of job roles increased efficiency while making it harder for workers to find meaning in their work. Reforms aimed at maximizing short-term flow ultimately moved toward consuming the social stock as a whole.[^nakagawa-work-meaning]

| Measure | Intended Rationality | Resulting Paradox |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prioritizing shareholder value | Improving capital efficiency and transparency | Excessive accumulation of retained earnings, suppression of human capital investment |
| Introducing quarterly reporting | Faster management and stronger disclosure | Avoidance of long-term market creation and R&D |
| Introducing capital efficiency metrics | Cultivating awareness of capital cost | Obstruction of innovative challenges |
| Fragmenting job roles | Productivity gains and specialization | Loss of meaning in work, deterioration of organizational ethics |
| Expanding non-regular employment | Flexible labor costs | Breaks in skill accumulation, erosion of the middle class and consumer base |

This table reconfigures four issues organized by Ryohei Nakagawa in "A Perspective on Antinomies of Corporate Behaviors after the 1990s in Japan": prioritization of shareholder ownership, short-termism, corporate rationalization, and changes in employment and work styles. It reframes them in the context of MonkuAi's civilizational design.[^nakagawa-framework]

## 4. Returning to the Infinite Game: A Survival Principle for the AI Age

As AI and ASI accelerate short-term optimization across all areas of society, the strategy humanity should adopt is a transition from finite games aimed at victory to infinite games aimed at continuing play.

ASI will overwhelm humans on finite-game metrics such as efficiency, speed, and cost. There are limits to competing with AI on the same field of process optimization. What humans will increasingly need to do is define value and meaning that AI cannot automatically define by itself.

In this sense, "using things for a long time," "not ending things too easily," and "regenerating what has broken into the next function" are not merely old virtues. They are foundational concepts for rewriting civilization's evaluation function in the ASI age.

However, we must not fall into mere idealism. For security reasons, a certain level of production foundations and industrial capacity needed for defense must be maintained. The important point is not to keep all of society running on a wartime logic of short-term mobilization, but to preserve the minimum foundations needed for defense while steering civilian domains thoroughly toward high-quality sustainability.

## 5. Institutional Redesign: Valuing Longevity, Repair, and Regeneration

To make the sensibility of mottainai into a survival strategy for civilization, moral appeals are not enough. Institutions must be designed so that repairing, using things longer, and regenerating them becomes economically rational in practice.

Current economic indicators and tax systems evaluate mass replacement with new goods as growth, while failing to adequately value repairing old things and using them longer. If optimization is handed over to ASI under these conditions, AI will accelerate society further in the direction of increasing consumption volume.

What is needed is the internalization of external costs into prices and the realization of absolute decoupling between economic growth and environmental burden.

### Five Institutional Shifts for Civilizational Continuity in the AI Age

1. **Renewing indicators: from GDP to natural and human capital stock indicators**

   Introduce indicators that measure not only GDP as a flow, but also the health of natural capital, human capital, and social capital. Evaluate environmental value and longevity not as resistance to price competition, but as civilizational competitiveness.

2. **Transforming taxation: incentive design for longevity and regeneration**

   Introduce tax reductions for repair and long-term use, and environmental burden taxes on products that encourage planned obsolescence. Institutionally change the structure in which replacement is cheaper than repair.

3. **Building legal frameworks: the right to repair and durability obligations**

   Establish consumers' right to repair what they own, and require durability, repairability, and replaceable parts at the product design stage. This is not merely environmental policy. It can become a foundation for next-generation product innovation.

4. **Investing in human capital: reconstructing meaning through skills**

   Redefine employment not as short-term labor procurement, but as long-term skill inheritance and community formation. Restore the meaning of work and organizational ethics through in-house training and skill accumulation.[^nakagawa-training]

5. **Regional circulation: building decentralized and self-reliant systems**

   Circulate energy and resources within regions and build decentralized living foundations that do not depend only on economies of scale. This also becomes a form of civilizational resilience against excessive centralized optimization by ASI.

## 6. Connection to MonkuAi

This civilization survival strategy connects especially to three of MonkuAi's five principles.

| MonkuAi Principle | Connection to This Document |
| --- | --- |
| Departure from zero-sum competition | Shift from short-term victory and maximization of consumption volume toward long-term reproduction of community, natural capital, and human capital. |
| Structural alignment | Do not leave goodwill and ethics trapped inside individual effort. Embed them into social structures through institutions, taxation, indicators, and rights design. |

This document also addresses the prior question of how human society should update its evaluation function before asking what ASI should optimize. Alignment in the ASI age is not only the control of AI. It is a problem of civilizational design that includes human indicators, institutions, desires, and time horizons.

## 7. Conclusion: Re-implementing Japanese Wisdom

Japan's true survival strategy in the ASI age is not emotional nostalgia. It is the institutional re-implementation of long-term values backed by economic rationality.

Upgrading the positive-sum governance and mottainai spirit that Japan once relinquished after defeat is no longer only an ethical issue. It is a strategic tool for escaping resource constraints and runaway short-term optimization by AI.

By making "using things longer," "keeping things going," and "passing broken things into their next form" the default form of rationality, Japan may be able to build a distinctive civilizational economic sphere that is not swallowed by price competition.

From quantity to quality. From consumption to inheritance. From short-term optimization to the infinite game.

This upgrade is an important solution for Japan, and for humanity, to survive the ASI age and continue playing richly.

## References and Notes

[^nakagawa-westernization]: Ryohei Nakagawa, "[A Perspective on Antinomies of Corporate Behaviors after the 1990s in Japan](https://www.ritsumei.ac.jp/ir/isaru/assets/file/journal/25-3_07_Ryohei_NAKAGAWA.pdf)," *Ritsumeikan International Affairs*, Vol. 25, No. 3, 2013. The paper analyzes how Japanese corporations after the 1990s gradually adjusted toward "Westernized" and "modernized" behavior, and how rational behavior by individual firms and households can produce paradoxical macro-level results through the fallacy of composition.

[^nakagawa-quarterly-eva]: The same paper discusses the shift toward shareholder value and short-termism, noting that quarterly reporting places clear three-month performance pressure on firms and can make R&D and long-term customer base formation lower priorities. It also describes EVA as increasing explainability to investors while strengthening short-term orientation; in the Sony case, it reduced room for innovative R&D.

[^nakagawa-work-meaning]: The paper argues that in a specialized and fragmented world of work, the search for "meaning in work" and self-realization can more easily fail. This document connects that discussion to the ASI-age problem of human capital and civilizational continuity.

[^nakagawa-framework]: Nakagawa organizes changes in Japanese corporations in the 1990s and 2000s around four elements: "shareholders' ownership," "short-termism," "corporate rationalization," and "employment and people's work style changes." He analyzes how these contain antinomies at long-term, macro, and labor-market levels.

[^nakagawa-training]: The paper explains that in-house training in Japanese firms was closely tied to long-term employment, and that the advance of short-termism and the increase in non-regular workers greatly lowered the rate of systematic OJT provision for non-regular workers compared with regular workers, producing long-term negative effects on growth.
